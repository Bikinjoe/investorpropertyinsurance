If a property sits vacant for more than 30 or 60 days, your landlord policy probably restricts or eliminates coverage for several common claim types. Most investors don't know this until they have a denied claim.

Here's the vacancy clause, how it actually works, and what to do about it.

## What the vacancy clause does

Most landlord (DP-3) policies include a vacancy clause that limits or excludes coverage once the property has been vacant for a specified period. The threshold is usually 30 or 60 days, depending on the carrier.

After the vacancy threshold, the policy may exclude coverage for:

- Vandalism
- Theft (including theft of installed property like copper plumbing)
- Water damage from plumbing freezes or burst pipes
- Glass breakage
- Certain types of malicious damage

The carrier still covers fire and certain other perils, but the most common vacancy claims are excluded. A frozen pipe in winter, a copper theft from an unoccupied property, vandalism while the property is on the market - these are exactly the things that happen during vacancies, and exactly the things the vacancy clause excludes.

## What counts as vacant

Vacancy isn't the same as unoccupied. A property between tenants with the previous tenant's belongings still inside isn't technically vacant. A property where someone is staying occasionally (a friend, a family member, a property manager) may not be vacant.

A property that is fully empty (no people, no furniture, no business activity) is vacant.

The carrier looks at this at claim time. If they conclude the property was vacant for the period preceding the loss, the vacancy clause applies.

## How to handle short vacancies (30 to 60 days)

For most tenant turnover, the gap is less than 30 days. Standard policy applies. Move on.

For vacancies that will run 30 to 60 days, talk to your agent. Some carriers don't trigger the clause until 60 days; some don't until 30. Knowing your specific threshold matters.

If you're going to push the threshold, ask for a vacancy permit endorsement. It's typically a small additional premium and keeps standard coverage in place during the vacant period.

## How to handle longer vacancies (60+ days)

Longer vacancies need a different policy. Two paths:

- **Vacancy permit endorsement** for vacancies up to 90 or 180 days, depending on the carrier. Premium adjustment is moderate.
- **Vacant property form** (typically a DP-1 dwelling form with vacancy and rehab coverage) for longer vacancies, properties going into renovation, or properties that will be unoccupied through the rest of the policy term.

Both are easy to add. The denied claim if you skip them is hard to recover from.

## Renovation triggers a different question

If the property is going to be vacant because you're renovating it, that's a different situation from "between tenants." Active renovation has its own coverage needs (vandalism, theft of materials, contractor liability). For major renovations, builders risk is the right form. For cosmetic rehabs, a vacant property form with renovation endorsement usually works.

## Practical guidance

- Tell your agent before the vacancy threshold is reached, not after
- Pay the policy premium during the vacancy gap. Letting the policy lapse to "save money" creates a bigger problem.
- Walk the property at least weekly during a vacancy. Carriers expect reasonable inspection.
- For longer vacancies or renovation, switch to the right form.

The premium for vacancy coverage is small. The denied claim is not.
